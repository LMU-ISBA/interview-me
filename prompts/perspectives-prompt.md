# The perspectives prompt

Use this after the interview, once you have a specification, or at least a
corrected REFLECT summary. It asks for five people who would see your plan
differently, lets you pick three, and then puts the three to work on the same
questions so you can compare them. You make the call.

Why five and then three, instead of "give me three experts." Picking is the
thinking. When you drop two, you are deciding which blind spots matter for
this decision. Asking each candidate which of the others they would argue
with forces real differences, so you do not get three cautious reviewers
saying the same thing in different hats.

This is not the old "act as an expert" move. A role does not make a model
more accurate (Zheng and others, 2024), and three voices from one model are
not independent. What the step gives you is positions you have to answer
before you commit. In a lab study, groups assigned a devil's advocate produced
better assumptions and recommendations than consensus groups, and liked it
less (Schweiger, Sandberg, and Ragan, 1986). We are borrowing the move.

## Part one, paste after your specification or summary

```
Here is where I have got to on a problem: my corrected summary or my plan, and the direction I am leaning. I wrote it. Propose five people who would see it differently from me. Use roles, not names. Give each a different thing they would protect, such as their time, fairness, evidence, morale, or reputation. For each, in three or four lines: who they are, what they would notice that I have not, what they would get wrong, and which of the other four they would disagree with most. Then stop. I will pick three and say why I dropped two. Do not argue with my choice.
```

## Part two, after you have chosen

```
Answer as each of my three, one at a time, to the same three questions: what is right about this plan, what breaks first, and what one change would you make. Keep each under 120 words and in their own priorities. Where two would agree, say so in the comparison rather than softening one to make room. Then write a comparison under three headings: where all three agree, where they split, and what none of them raised. Stop after the third heading. No conclusion, no recommendation, no next step. Then ask me one question: which change, if any, I want to make to the plan.
```

## Why it's worded this way

- The two parts are separate on purpose. Pasted together, the model picks the
  three itself and the choosing step disappears.
- "Give each a different thing they would protect" is what keeps the five
  from converging into three cautious reviewers saying the same thing.
- "Stop after the third heading" and the closing question replace "do not
  recommend," which models ignore because a comparison feels unfinished
  without a verdict.
